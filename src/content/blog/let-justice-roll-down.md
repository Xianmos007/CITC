There are certain verses in Scripture that seem to echo through generations. Amos 5:24 is one of them:

> "But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream."

If we are honest, verses like this can make many pastors nervous. In today's culture, words like justice have become heavily politicized. Depending on who hears the word, they may immediately associate it with a political ideology, a movement, or a culture war. But when Amos speaks about justice, he is not talking about partisan talking points. He is talking about the fruit of genuine worship.

In Amos 5, God rebukes Israel because their worship and their lives no longer matched. The people were still gathering, singing, sacrificing, and celebrating religious feasts, but while the temple was full of worshippers, injustice filled the land. The poor were oppressed. The vulnerable were ignored. The legal system had become corrupt. Their songs sounded beautiful to one another, but God said, "Take away from me the noise of your songs." It is a terrifying picture because the issue was not that Israel lacked religious activity. The issue was that their lives contradicted their worship.

Then comes Amos 5:24: "But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream." Throughout Scripture, justice and righteousness are deeply connected ideas. They are often paired together because they are really two sides of the same coin. Righteousness is covenant faithfulness expressed vertically and personally, while justice is covenant faithfulness expressed horizontally and socially. Or to put it more simply, genuine relationship with God changes both our hearts and the way we treat people.

This is why local churches should care deeply about serving their communities. Not because we are trying to earn God's favor, build a platform, or look impressive, but because transformed people naturally begin reflecting the heart of Christ. The gospel changes our motivation completely. We do not serve vulnerable families hoping God will finally love us. We serve because in Christ we have already been fully loved. We do not pursue justice to prove ourselves righteous. We pursue justice because we belong to the Righteous One.

That is one of the reasons I am so grateful for Church in the City. One of the things I appreciate most about this ministry is that it gives local churches practical opportunities to live out Amos 5:24 together. It helps connect churches to real needs in our communities and reminds us that justice is not merely an abstract idea or a sermon topic. It looks like mentoring vulnerable families, feeding children who are food insecure, serving the homeless, supporting foster and adoptive families, and stepping into broken places with compassion.

Amos says justice should roll down like waters and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream. In other words, this is not something we occasionally participate in when it is convenient or emotionally moving. It is meant to become part of the culture of God's people. And this is where the local church shines. No organization on earth is better positioned to sustain long-term compassion, generosity, and service than the local church empowered by the Holy Spirit.

Every church has unique giftings, unique people, and unique opportunities. Some churches may be equipped to mentor families. Others may serve through food distribution, foster care support, addiction recovery, discipleship, counseling, tutoring, or homeless outreach. The opportunities are endless because every community bears the marks of both brokenness and the image of God. Church in the City simply helps weave those efforts together.

As pastors, it can be easy for us to feel overwhelmed by the needs around us. We cannot solve every problem in our city, but we can faithfully ask where God has positioned our church to help mend the fabric of justice. That question matters because when the church lives this way, our worship begins to align with our lives. Our songs are no longer disconnected from our obedience. The gospel becomes visible.

Ultimately, that is what Amos was calling Israel back to. Not empty religion, performative worship, or outward appearances, but hearts transformed by God that overflow into righteousness and justice.

My encouragement to local pastors is simple: don't let the politicization of the word justice rob the church of a deeply biblical calling. Amos was not the first prophet to speak about justice, and he certainly was not the last. Isaiah told God's people to "seek justice, correct oppression." Micah said that the Lord requires us "to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God." Jesus Himself rebuked the Pharisees for neglecting "the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faithfulness."

Biblical justice is not partisan, performative, or transactional. It is simply the fruit of a people who have been transformed by grace. So let justice roll down like waters. And maybe one of the ways God wants that to happen in our city is through churches partnering together for the good of our neighbors and the glory of Christ.
